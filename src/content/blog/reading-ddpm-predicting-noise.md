For today's blog post, I'm going to be going over the paper [*Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239) by Ho et al. (2020). It represents a highly influential approach to generative modeling.

The overarching concept is to train a neural network to predict the noise that has been added to an image. By learning to remove this noise, the model can eventually generate entirely new images starting from random noise.

## The Big Picture

Imagine having a dataset containing thousands of cat images. You want to train a model to generate a completely new image of a cat, even though that particular cat has **NEVER** appeared in the training dataset.

DDPM approaches this through a two-step process:

1. **Forward diffusion:** Gradually adding Gaussian noise to real images until they become almost indistinguishable from random noise.
2. **Reverse diffusion:** Training a neural network to reverse that corruption process, removing noise one step at a time.

What's clever about this is that the model doesn't have to learn how to generate a new cat image in one operation. Instead, it learns a much simpler task: given a noisy image, predict the noise that was added.

By repeating this operation hundreds or thousands of times, random noise can gradually become a realistic image.

<div class="callout" role="note">

**Note:** Since this paper came out, several methods have been developed to speed up diffusion-based generation, such as distillation (teacher model to student model) and DDIM, which can generate images using fewer sampling steps. There is also **Flow Matching**, a related generative modeling approach that learns a vector field to transport samples from a simple distribution, such as Gaussian noise, toward the data distribution. Some flow-matching formulations use straight-line interpolation between noise and data samples.

</div>

## The Forward Diffusion Process

This is a fixed mathematical procedure. At each timestep $t$, we add a small amount of Gaussian noise.

As you can imagine, this process could end up giving us hundreds, thousands, or even more timesteps.

The authors used a clever mathematical property of Gaussian distributions that allows us to directly access any timestep without having to generate all the previous ones. The forward process is also modeled as a Markov chain, although I won't be going into the mathematical details in this blog post.

This is a very important concept for efficient training.

Instead of generating $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{500}$ just to train on timestep 500, we can directly construct $x_{500}$ from the original image.

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon
$$

where $\epsilon$ is sampled from a standard Gaussian distribution.

Breaking down what each piece is:

- $x_0$ is the original clean image.
- $x_t$ is the noisy version of it at timestep $t$.
- $\epsilon$ is the random noise we sampled, the same shape as the image.
- $\bar\alpha_t$ ("alpha bar") comes from the noise schedule. It starts close to 1 and shrinks as $t$ grows.

The two square roots work like a pair of dials that always stay balanced. $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ is how much of the original image survives, and $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ is how much noise gets mixed in. Early on, when $\bar\alpha_t$ is near 1, the image dominates and you can still tell what it is. By the last timestep it's near 0, so there's basically nothing left but noise.

That's the whole trick: pick a timestep, look up its $\bar\alpha_t$, and you can mix the image and the noise in one shot.

## The Reverse Diffusion Process

> Now this is where the fun begins.

Suppose we have a noisy image $x_t$, and we want to predict a slightly cleaner image $x_{t-1}$.

DDPM learns a reverse probability distribution, where the model receives two inputs:

- The noisy image $x_t$.
- The timestep $t$, which tells it how much corruption to expect.

In the original DDPM implementation, the neural network is based on a U-Net architecture.

Instead of directly predicting the clean image, the model is trained to predict the noise present in the image.

Once the model predicts the noise, we can use that estimate to calculate the mean of the reverse-step distribution (remember, it's Gaussian). We then sample from that Gaussian probability distribution to obtain the next, slightly cleaner image.

This process repeats until we reach the final generated image $x_0$.

## The Loss Function

Where would we be if we didn't talk about the loss function?

The authors derived a training objective from a variational lower bound on data likelihood, but the simplified objective they use is essentially the squared error between the actual noise and the predicted noise.

Consider a single training example:

1. Select a real image from the training dataset.
2. Randomly select a timestep, such as $t = 500$.
3. Generate a random Gaussian noise tensor $\epsilon$.
4. Add the appropriate amount of noise to the image to produce $x_{500}$.
   - Up until here, this is the forward diffusion process.
5. Feed $x_{500}$ and $t = 500$ into the neural network.
6. Compare the predicted noise against the actual noise using mean squared error (MSE).
7. Backpropagate the error and update the neural network's weights.

## Now We Finally Come to the Question: How Does the Model Generate Something Entirely New??

Well, once training is finished, you don't need the original image anymore.

You can start by sampling pure Gaussian noise, and the trained neural network will repeatedly predict noise and use it to calculate the next reverse step.

Because the model has learned statistical patterns from the training dataset, its denoising predictions tend to guide random noise toward realistic images.

## One Last Interesting Tidbit From Paper Club

Something interesting I learned during our paper club discussion was how the size of the denoising steps can affect the model's predictions.

If we use fewer timesteps or make larger jumps between noise levels, each denoising step can become more difficult because the model has to account for a greater amount of uncertainty.

This also connects to an interesting property of the MSE loss function.

When a model is uncertain and multiple predictions are plausible, minimizing MSE encourages it to predict the conditional average of those possibilities. In image prediction, averaging multiple plausible outcomes can sometimes result in blurry-looking predictions.

The intuition is that making an extreme prediction when you're uncertain can lead to a large squared error, so the model may favor a more conservative, averaged prediction.
